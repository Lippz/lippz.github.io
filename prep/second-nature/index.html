1 · Introduction

Could you briefly introduce yourself and describe your recent product design experience?

Hi Ami, I'm Pylyp, a product designer based in Poznań, Poland. My recent work at Existek focused on LinkHMS, a healthcare product with complex clinical and administrative workflows.

I worked across discovery, user interviews, UI design, prototyping, design systems and delivery, closely with business analysts and engineers. I also used Claude Code and Codex to build working prototypes and help implement components in the product.

2 · End-to-end ownership

Could you walk me through a complex feature you owned from discovery to launch?

One example is the partogram feature I designed for LinkHMS. It helps clinicians record and monitor the progress of labour.

I started by reviewing completed paper forms and asking clinicians to walk me through their workflow. The challenge was adapting that workflow to a digital interface.

I separated a quick overview from the detailed history. At the top, clinicians can see the latest observations. Below that, the charts share a common timeline, so they can compare changes over time.

I shared early sketches with clinicians, collected feedback, and refined the design over several rounds. I then prepared the detailed designs in Figma and worked with engineers on implementation. The feature was later released.

Portfolio presentation: the problem

LinkHMS is a clinic management system for small clinics in Nigeria and Ghana. The clinics we were designing for were still using paper records. We wanted to bring their day-to-day work into one digital system, including patient records, appointments and clinic administration.

Portfolio presentation: my role and user research

I was responsible for the UX and UI, and I spoke with nurses, doctors and receptionists to understand their day-to-day work. They described how difficult and inconvenient it was to maintain records and fill in patient information manually.

We included voice input for some documentation tasks, such as entering medical history, so users could dictate the information instead of typing everything.

Portfolio presentation: decisions shaped by working conditions

Speed of documentation was important. We included voice input for tasks such as recording medical history, so clinicians could dictate information rather than enter everything manually.

Another constraint was frequent power cuts. Staff used desktop computers and could lose information they had entered but not saved. We added autosave on many pages of the electronic medical record to reduce reliance on remembering to save manually.

These decisions responded to the conditions people actually worked in: helping them record information quickly and reducing the risk of losing unsaved work.

Portfolio presentation: outcome and lesson

Voice input and autosave became important selling points when we introduced LinkHMS to early customers. They addressed practical difficulties in daily documentation and were part of how we communicated the product's value.

I do not have a separate measurement of how much each feature contributed to adoption. The main lesson for me was to listen closely to users and understand their working conditions. Constraints such as power cuts can shape product decisions just as much as the workflow itself.

Screen walkthrough

Looking at this partogram screen, what does the clinician need to understand first, and why did you organise the information this way?

The top of the screen gives clinicians the context: when active labour started, how much time has passed, and the current phase.

Below that are eight sections for recording observations that populate the charts. I arranged them in the order clinicians were used to when completing the paper chart, so the digital workflow would feel familiar.

The charts below show changes over time on a shared timeline. Medication records and labour notes follow underneath. Notes can also be entered near the top of the page.

The structure moves from the current context and recording observations to reviewing the history and supporting details.

3 · User need and prioritisation

How did you identify the problem, and why did you address it?

We initially built a labour section in the patient's electronic medical record because customers needed it. Their feedback showed that it wasn't enough to support the full labour workflow.

I asked what was missing, and they showed us the paper partograms they used. That gave us a concrete gap to address: bringing that workflow into the digital product. I then reviewed the completed forms with them to understand how they recorded and used the information.

4 · Feedback and iteration

What feedback did you receive, and how did you improve the design?

The feedback showed that clinicians still approached the chart like the paper version. It wasn't clear to them that entering observations in the sections above would update the chart.

We added a small tooltip next to the input sections to clarify that entering data there updates the chart.

5 · Personal contribution and engineering

What was your responsibility, and how did you work with engineers?

I was responsible for the UX and UI of the feature. I reviewed the paper forms with clinicians, created early sketches, and collected feedback from potential users. I then prepared the detailed designs in Figma and shared them with the engineers.

They pointed out technical limitations, and I adjusted the design so we could implement the feature.

6 · AI in design

How have you used AI tools in your design work?

I use AI to brainstorm ideas for features and organise information. More recently, I've used Claude Code and Codex to maintain and improve our product's design system. So my use of AI has expanded from exploring ideas to working on the implementation as well.

7 · AI and efficiency

Can you give an example of how AI helped you work faster?

One example was updating an outdated button component across the product. I prepared the new version, then used an AI coding agent to replace the old button instances throughout the project. I defined the component, and the agent helped with the repetitive migration work.

8 · Checking AI output

How did you check that the AI-assisted changes were correct?

I asked a second agent to review the changes. I also checked the interface visually myself to make sure the old buttons had been replaced correctly.

9 · Development readiness

How do you decide when a design is ready for development?

I use a checklist throughout the design process, rather than leaving all the checks until handoff. I check that the interactions are clear and that the flow includes the necessary screens and modals.

I also check consistency with the design system, including colours, styles, spacing and design tokens. For me, readiness means that the flow is clear and the designs include what engineers need to implement it.

10 · Behaviour-driven improvement

Can you tell me about a design you improved after looking at user behaviour?

In LinkHMS, I used Microsoft Clarity to review how people interacted with the date picker in the scheduler. I noticed signs that users were struggling to switch dates. Receptionists were the main users of that screen.

I moved the Today button outside the date picker and added left and right arrows for quick navigation between days.

After the change, I observed fewer repeated clicks and less time spent finding a date. Our on-site partners also gave positive feedback. These were observations rather than a controlled experiment, so I don't have a precise percentage improvement.

11 · Motivation

Why are you interested in Second Nature, and how is your experience relevant?

I follow developments in AI closely, and I'd like to work on a product where it solves a clear user problem. What interests me about Second Nature is using AI to help people practise conversations and get feedback.

My experience is in making complex workflows clearer and easier to use. I'd like to bring that experience to an AI product and learn more about designing these interactions.

12 · Design decisions

What alternatives did you consider for an important design decision, and why did you choose the final approach?

We started LinkHMS as an MVP, with limited time and budget. In many cases, the decision was whether to reuse an existing solution or invest in a more customised one.

Early on, I often reused existing components to get the initial version delivered. As we learned more from users, I refined those solutions where they weren't meeting their needs.

So the approach changed as the product developed: first, work within our constraints, then use feedback to decide what needed further improvement.

13 · Complex workflows

How have you simplified a complex workflow involving different roles, permissions or large amounts of information?

In LinkHMS, we started with the administrator role, which had access to all modules and actions. I then used those designs as the basis for other clinic roles, with access limited to the relevant modules and functions.

I organised the designs in Figma by role to show what each role could access. I also designed a permissions screen in the clinic administrator's workflow, where access could be configured.

We later added permissions for individual users as well. For example, a nurse who also booked appointments could be given access to the scheduler without changing permissions for every nurse.

This combined role-based access with flexibility for people whose responsibilities went beyond their standard role.

14 · Production UI

How do you handle interface states and responsive behaviour when preparing a feature for production?

I create separate screens for different interface states and connect them in Figma to show what happens after each user action.

LinkHMS was mainly designed for desktop because most users worked on computers. We also designed a small set of mobile screens for nurses to record basic updates, such as logging a vaccination that had been given or entering a patient's vital signs.

For tables on smaller screens, we used horizontal scrolling. Where tables included actions, we kept those actions pinned on the right so users could access them without scrolling all the way across.

15 · Design systems

What was your role in building or maintaining the design system, and how did you keep it consistent?

I created the UI kit that later became the basis of the LinkHMS design system. I originally built it for an earlier project. Since our company worked on different client products, reusing that foundation helped us work within tight time and budget constraints.

For LinkHMS, I adapted and gradually updated the components to fit the product's needs. I used Figma variables as design tokens and connected them to component properties to keep styling consistent.

I also helped another designer learn the system. I explained how the components and variables worked and how to adjust their appearance using the existing design tokens.

16 · Prototyping and learning

How do you use quick prototypes and user feedback to test an idea before investing in detailed design?

My approach changed as the product developed. At the beginning, I used wireframes because they were the quickest way to work through the structure.

Once the design system was ready, I usually built the screens directly from existing components. That was faster than creating a separate set of low-fidelity wireframes.

I used those designs to collect user feedback, then refined the details. So I didn't always have a separate quick-prototyping stage. The design system helped me get to something we could discuss with users quickly.

17 · Team collaboration

How do you work with a PM or business analyst to clarify requirements and handle feedback or disagreement?

I keep an ongoing dialogue with the business analyst about requirements and priorities. We discuss what is essential and what can be left out. I explain my reasoning about the importance of a feature and how it should work, and listen to their perspective as well.

We also collect user feedback together and use it to decide what to do next. If we disagree, I try to understand the reasoning behind their view and explain mine so we can work towards a shared decision.

18 · Ambiguity and delivery

How do you move forward when requirements are unclear or time is limited?

I use an LLM to explore ideas, and I discuss them with the business analyst to clarify what we need to achieve. Together, we look for a simple approach that addresses the main need within the time available.

I then prepare an initial version, collect feedback, and refine it. That helps us make progress without trying to resolve every detail upfront.

19 · Success criteria

How do you define success for a feature when you do not yet have reliable quantitative data?

Without reliable quantitative data, I focus on whether users understand the feature and can complete the task it was designed for.

After launch, I review user behaviour in Microsoft Clarity and collect feedback. Our clients were generally open and willing to share their experiences. I also followed up with them and asked directly what they found frustrating, rather than waiting for complaints. We then used that feedback to guide improvements.

These observations help me decide what needs improvement, but I wouldn't claim a measured increase in performance without reliable numbers.

20 · Practical task

If you were given an unfamiliar product problem, how would you approach it from clarification to an initial solution?

First, I would clarify who the users are, what they are trying to achieve, and what problem we need to solve. I would also ask about the business goal, the available information, and any technical or time constraints.

Then I would review the existing workflow and look at how competitors or similar products approach the problem. I would use that as a reference, rather than assume their solution fits our product.

From there, I would sketch an initial approach, discuss it with the team, and get user feedback where possible. I would refine the solution based on what we learn.
